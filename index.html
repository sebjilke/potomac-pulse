What Is Potomac Pulse?

There's no gauge at Great Falls. Potomac Pulse estimates what's happening there right now — and what's coming — so you can plan your time on the water.

Reading Your Screen

CFS — Cubic feet per second. How much water is flowing. Higher = more water, bigger features.

Stage — Water height in feet on the Little Falls scale, so you can compare to levels you already know.

Confidence — How much data the estimate is based on.

● High — Multiple sources agree. Number is solid — plan around it.
● Medium — Some data gaps. Reasonable, but could shift 10-20%.
● Low — Limited data. Treat as ballpark — check back in an hour.

How Accurate Is It?

Every prediction gets checked ~6 hours later when that water reaches Little Falls. The system learns correction factors for different conditions and gets better over time.

Best for: Steady or slowly changing conditions — planning a weekend trip
Good for: Rising water and storm pulses — watching a wave move through the basin
Least reliable: Ice conditions, gauge malfunctions, extreme floods (>100k cfs)
This is an estimate, not a measurement
Don't use it for life-safety decisions. Localized storms between gauges won't show until that water arrives, and fast-rising floods may arrive sooner than predicted. Always check NWS warnings independently.

The blend is smart: At low flows, local channel effects make Edwards Ferry a poor downstream predictor, so the model uses Point of Rocks alone. As flows increase, EF weight rises smoothly via a logistic function (0% → 40%, midpoint at 10k cfs) — calibrated on 117,000+ hourly observations (2011–2026) and cross-verified in Python and R.

UNDER THE HOOD

The logistic blend was selected from seven competing approaches via leave-one-year-out cross-validation. Typical prediction error is ~6%, varying by flow: higher below 3,000 cfs, under 9% above 15,000 cfs.

EF power-law: 126 × EF2.46 (default) or 160 × EF2.36 (cold water ≤10°C / 50°F).
Safety cap: Estimate capped at 120% of Little Falls actual to prevent runaway overshoot.
Flow state: Rising/falling/steady detected from observed PoR rate (6-hour lookback), not forecasts.

The 48-Hour Forecast

The forecast uses official NWS predictions for the Potomac, adjusted to match what the gauges actually read right now. We shift the NWS timeline to account for how long water takes to travel between gauges — so you see when conditions will change at Great Falls.

This works differently than the "now" estimate. Since water takes so long to travel from Point of Rocks, we take the Little Falls forecast and work backwards — if Little Falls will rise, Great Falls must rise first.

How Fast Does Water Travel?

This is the key to the whole system. Water takes hours to flow downstream, so today's Point of Rocks reading tells you what arrives at Great Falls tomorrow. To estimate GF right now, we look at what Point of Rocks was reading a day ago.

Flow LevelSpeedPt of Rocks → Little Falls
Very low (~1.2k cfs)~0.9 mph~44 hrs
Low (~2k cfs)~1.2 mph~35 hrs
Typical (~5k cfs)~1.6 mph~26 hrs
High (~15k cfs)~3 mph~14 hrs
Flood (~50k+ cfs)~6 mph~7 hrs

Based on USGS Circular 438 (Searcy, 1961), updated with modern 15-minute gauge data. Water now travels ~20% faster than the 1960s model predicted.

Basin Coverage

19 USGS gauges feed the model. Point of Rocks carries about 84% of the flow, with Monocacy, Goose Creek, Broad Run, and Seneca Creek adding the rest. Edwards Ferry provides a nearby cross-check just 2 miles above Great Falls. Upstream gauges (Hancock, Cumberland, Shenandoah) give multi-day advance notice when a pulse is coming.

Data Sources

Real-time gauges: USGS Water Services (updates every 15 min)
Forecasts: National Weather Service river predictions
Water temperature: USGS Point of Rocks gauge

Creeks Tab

The Creeks tab shows rain-dependent whitewater runs near DC. These use separate USGS gauges — a creek shows green when flow is at or above its runnable threshold (e.g., Rock Creek ≥400 cfs). This is display-only and does not affect the Great Falls estimate.

The Washington Aqueduct, WSSC, and Fairfax Water all divert water above Great Falls — roughly 400–700 cfs combined. This water never goes over the falls. The estimate shown is calibrated to Little Falls (downstream), so these diversions are already built into the model. Ice can corrupt gauge readings — the system detects anomalies and flags suspicious data automatically.
